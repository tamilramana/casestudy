# Literature Survey: IoT Botnet Detection Using Anomaly Detection

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of internet-connected consumer devices — cameras, doorbells, smart plugs, baby monitors — has created a large, weakly secured attack surface. Unlike traditional endpoints, IoT devices are typically resource-constrained, run minimal or outdated firmware, and are rarely patched, which makes them attractive targets for large-scale botnet malware such as Mirai and BASHLITE/Gafgyt. This survey reviews the literature most relevant to a project built around the N-BaIoT dataset, covering (1) the origin and structure of that dataset, (2) autoencoder-based anomaly detection, (3) Isolation Forest and other lightweight unsupervised methods, (4) comparative studies between these approaches, (5) the edge-deployment and resource-efficiency angle that is distinctive to IoT security research, and (6) broader intrusion-detection literature that contextualizes this work. It closes with a summary of open challenges relevant to this project's design choices.

## 2. The N-BaIoT Dataset and Its Origin

The N-BaIoT dataset originates from a study by Meidan et al. (2018), published in *IEEE Pervasive Computing*, which proposed a network-based anomaly detection method for IoT specifically because compromised IoT devices needed detection methods capable of distinguishing both hour-long and millisecond-long attacks. The authors infected nine commercial IoT devices in a lab setting with two widely known IoT botnets, Mirai and BASHLITE, and demonstrated that their method could accurately and near-instantly detect attacks as they were launched from the compromised devices (Meidan et al., 2018, arXiv:1805.03409).

Methodologically, the original study trained a separate deep autoencoder per device using only benign traffic — specifically, two-thirds of each device's benign data was used for training, with the remaining third combined with all malicious traffic for testing (Meidan et al., 2018; UCI/Kaggle dataset documentation). Each per-device traffic snapshot is summarized using 115 statistical features aggregated over multiple time windows, capturing properties such as packet size, rate, and direction (SNU MMLab reproduction, n.d.; arXiv:1905.07107). Later work quantifying the dataset's scale reports roughly 7.06 million total instances across the nine devices, of which only about 556,000 are benign and the remainder are malicious, split across ten distinct attack vectors from the Mirai and Gafgyt families (arXiv:2607.04698, 2026) — figures that underline just how heavily attack traffic dominates once an infection is underway, which is a very different imbalance profile from the fraud-detection setting.

This project's dataset choice is therefore well grounded: it is real infected-device traffic rather than simulated data, it is pre-aggregated into device-level statistical features (removing the need to process raw packet captures), and it is one of the most heavily benchmarked datasets in IoT security research (ResearchGate reproduction discussion, 2026).

## 3. Autoencoder-Based Anomaly Detection

The autoencoder-only-on-benign-traffic approach central to this project's Step 2 (Option A) is precisely the technique introduced in the original N-BaIoT paper: an autoencoder is trained exclusively on normal traffic so that it learns to compress and reconstruct normal patterns well, and any traffic that reconstructs poorly (i.e., produces a high reconstruction error) is flagged as anomalous (Meidan et al., 2018). Follow-up work by an overlapping set of authors extended this idea into Kitsune, an ensemble of small autoencoders designed for online, real-time network intrusion detection rather than a single large offline model (cited in arXiv:2010.11453, 2020).

Subsequent literature has both validated and complicated this picture. On one hand, deep autoencoders, recurrent neural networks, and convolutional architectures have repeatedly been shown to achieve strong detection rates on N-BaIoT-style traffic. On the other hand, more recent reviews explicitly flag that these deep learning approaches bring significant computational overhead, longer training times, and heavier memory footprints — a critical bottleneck for continuous, real-time deployment directly on resource-constrained IoT edge nodes (ResearchGate, citing recent survey discussion, 2026). This tension between detection accuracy and deployability is one of the central themes running through the more recent literature, and it motivates this project's inclusion of a lighter-weight baseline (Isolation Forest / statistical thresholding) alongside the autoencoder.

Autoencoders have also been extended in more specialized directions relevant to possible project extensions: sequential-hypothesis-testing variants aim to detect compromised devices faster by combining autoencoder reconstruction error with statistical decision rules rather than a single fixed threshold (arXiv:2404.13690, n.d.), and federated variants (e.g., conditional variational autoencoders) have been proposed specifically to train per-device or per-organization models without centrally pooling raw traffic data, addressing privacy concerns analogous to those raised in the credit-card-fraud federated-learning literature (arXiv:2607.04698, 2026).

## 4. Isolation Forest and Other Lightweight Unsupervised Methods

Isolation Forest, proposed as this project's Option B, works by recursively and randomly partitioning the feature space; because anomalous points are "few and different," they tend to require fewer partitioning steps to isolate than normal points, which are more numerous and more tightly clustered. A key practical advantage repeatedly noted in the literature is that Isolation Forest does not rely on distance computations, which reduces its computational cost relative to autoencoders and other neural approaches (arXiv:2204.03779, n.d.).

One-Class SVM is another unsupervised baseline used in adjacent IoT security work. A 2025 study on the TON_IoT dataset comparing Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM, and a lightweight fusion method found that One-Class SVM achieved superior precision, recall, and accuracy compared to both Isolation Forest and the fusion approach in that particular setting, illustrating that the relative ranking of these lightweight unsupervised methods is not fixed and depends on the dataset and traffic characteristics (Zahoor et al., 2025, *Scientific Reports*).

## 5. Comparative Studies: Autoencoder vs. Isolation Forest vs. Statistical Baselines

Because this project's Step 2 explicitly proposes comparing Options A, B, and C, the comparative literature is especially relevant. Several recent studies run exactly this kind of comparison:

- A Brazilian study directly compared Isolation Forest, Autoencoder, and Random Forest for anomaly detection in home IoT network traffic, evaluating both detection performance and computational resource consumption across two public datasets, and found that unsupervised models could achieve competitive detection performance without the resource overhead of more complex supervised models (SBC/UESB study, n.d.).
- A separate comparative study evaluating Isolation Forest, Autoencoder, and an Ensemble Voting approach under a temporally separated (more realistic) train/test split found that Isolation Forest achieved the best overall balance of metrics (78.7% accuracy, 0.82 precision, 0.79 recall, 0.80 F1, 0.78 AUC), outperforming the standalone Autoencoder while requiring lower computational complexity; the Ensemble Voting approach performed similarly to Isolation Forest alone, suggesting the Autoencoder's marginal contribution was small once Isolation Forest was included (Research Square preprint, 2025).
- A hybrid framework called EcoDefender combined autoencoder-based feature learning with Isolation Forest-based anomaly scoring specifically to balance accuracy against energy and latency constraints, reporting up to 94% detection accuracy with substantially lower CPU usage, inference latency, and energy consumption than an autoencoder-only baseline (Jamshidi et al., 2025, arXiv:2511.18235).
- A 2025 survey of embedded/edge anomaly detection methods (covering Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM, recurrent architectures, and statistical techniques) concluded that the choice of algorithm is fundamentally shaped by hardware constraints — latency, memory, and power — and not by accuracy alone, reinforcing that "best" is deployment-context-dependent rather than universal (Benmachiche et al., 2025, arXiv:2512.19383).

Collectively, this comparative literature suggests that Isolation Forest is a genuinely competitive — and sometimes superior — baseline relative to autoencoders on IoT anomaly detection tasks, particularly once realistic (temporally separated) evaluation and computational cost are taken into account, which validates this project's plan to treat Options A–C as peers to be benchmarked rather than assuming the neural network is automatically best.

## 6. Edge Deployment and Resource Efficiency

A recurring theme distinguishing IoT botnet detection from many other anomaly-detection domains is that the detector itself often has to run on constrained hardware close to the devices it protects. One lightweight autoencoder-based intrusion detection approach for Industrial IoT reported being the first to deploy its model on a Jetson Nano edge device, achieving sub-millisecond inference times (0.185 ms for binary classification) while comparing favorably against several recent techniques on accuracy and F1-score (arXiv:2501.15266, 2025). This "accuracy is necessary but not sufficient" framing — where papers report not just Precision/Recall/F1 but also training time, inference time, CPU usage, and energy consumption — is now common across the lightweight-IDS literature (Jamshidi et al., 2025; arXiv:2501.15266). This directly supports the "Optional deployment" idea in this project's tech stack section and suggests that, for a stronger project, reporting inference latency and resource usage alongside detection metrics would align well with current research norms.

## 7. Broader Intrusion Detection Context

Beyond N-BaIoT-specific work, this project connects to the broader network intrusion detection (NIDS) literature. Bidirectional LSTM architectures have been proposed for intrusion detection more generally, aiming to capture sequential dependencies in traffic the way LSTM-based fraud detection captures sequential transaction behavior (Imrana et al., 2021, cited in arXiv:2604.11324). More recent work has also begun applying transformer architectures to IoT intrusion detection (Akuthota & Bhargava, 2025, cited in arXiv:2604.11324), and cross-domain benchmarks are emerging that test whether a detector trained on one IoT deployment generalizes to a structurally different one (arXiv:2604.11324, "BRIDGE and TCH-Net"). In IoT-adjacent domains such as the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), similar behavioral, lightweight anomaly-detection approaches have been proposed because heavy cryptographic or computational defenses are impractical on battery-constrained medical devices — for example, a pacemaker cannot afford the power cost of heavy encryption, reinforcing why lightweight statistical and unsupervised methods are preferred over heavier alternatives in resource-constrained settings generally (THESAI/IJACSA, 2026).

## 8. Summary of Open Challenges Identified in the Literature

- **Accuracy alone is an incomplete evaluation criterion.** A growing share of recent papers argue that detection metrics must be reported alongside computational cost (latency, memory, CPU, energy), since a highly accurate but heavy autoencoder may be undeployable on real IoT edge hardware.
- **The relative ranking of Autoencoder vs. Isolation Forest vs. One-Class SVM is not fixed** — it depends on the dataset, the realism of the train/test split (especially whether temporal separation is respected), and whether resource constraints are part of the evaluation criteria.
- **Per-device modeling is well established as necessary**, consistent with this project's Step 1 approach of keeping normal-traffic profiles separate per device type, since the original N-BaIoT methodology itself trained a distinct autoencoder for each of the nine devices.
- **Evaluation realism matters.** Studies that use a temporally separated train/test split (mimicking real deployment) sometimes reach different conclusions than studies using randomly shuffled splits, echoing a caution seen in the fraud-detection literature about overly optimistic results from unrealistic evaluation setups.
- **Privacy-preserving and federated variants** are an active research direction for organizations that want to benefit from shared anomaly-detection models without pooling raw device traffic centrally.
- **The field is moving toward hybrid architectures** (e.g., autoencoder feature learning feeding into Isolation Forest scoring) rather than treating each method as a standalone competitor, which is a natural next step beyond this project's proposed "compare A vs. B vs. C" extension.
