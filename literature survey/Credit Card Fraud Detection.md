# Literature Survey: Credit Card Fraud Detection Using Machine Learning

## 1. Introduction

Credit card fraud detection has become one of the most widely studied applied machine learning problems, largely because it combines a real business need (financial institutions lose significant sums to fraud) with a genuinely hard technical challenge: extreme class imbalance, evolving fraud patterns, and the need for models whose decisions can be justified to auditors and customers alike. This survey reviews the main streams of research relevant to a project built around the Kaggle "Credit Card Fraud Detection" (European cardholders, 2013) dataset, covering (1) general surveys of the field, (2) classical supervised learning approaches, (3) techniques for handling class imbalance, (4) ensemble and boosting methods, (5) deep learning approaches, and (6) explainability. It closes with a summary of open challenges that motivate the design choices in this project.

## 2. Broad Surveys of the Field

Several systematic reviews map out the overall shape of the field. A 2023 systematic review synthesized 181 articles published between 2019 and 2021 on machine learning and deep learning approaches to credit card fraud, concluding that traditional rule-based and anomaly-detection systems are increasingly being replaced by learning-based methods, while flagging class imbalance, concept drift, and limited labeled data as persistent open problems (Btoush et al., 2023).

A related bibliometric review of financial fraud detection literature found that decision trees, support vector machines, and general "machine learning" methods are the most frequently recurring themes across the literature, with a strong concentration of work specifically on credit card fraud relative to other fraud types such as insurance or loan fraud (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2024). A further review focused specifically on comparative evaluation of ML techniques for credit card fraud detection, ultimately proposing a hybrid solution based on an artificial neural network trained within a federated learning framework, aimed at addressing data-confidentiality constraints that arise when banks cannot centrally pool transaction data (Springer HCIS, 2022).

Together, these surveys establish that the field has matured from ad hoc comparisons of individual algorithms toward more systematic benchmarking, and that class imbalance and interpretability are now treated as first-class concerns rather than afterthoughts.

## 3. Classical Supervised Learning Baselines

Early and still widely used baselines include Logistic Regression, k-Nearest Neighbors, Naive Bayes, Decision Trees, and Support Vector Machines. A comparative study using the same Kaggle dataset used in this project (284,807 transactions from European cardholders) evaluated Logistic Regression, Bayesian Networks, k-NN (with k = 3 and k = 7), Naive Bayes, and SVM, finding that Logistic Regression performed best overall in that particular literature comparison, while Bayesian Networks were notably fast at identifying fraudulent transactions with high accuracy (RIT thesis repository, n.d.).

A widely cited IEEE conference paper directly compared Naive Bayes, k-NN, and Logistic Regression on the same highly skewed dataset, reinforcing that simple models can serve as reasonable baselines but tend to struggle as the imbalance ratio becomes extreme (IEEE, "A comparative analysis," n.d.). These simpler models remain valuable in this project as interpretable baselines against which more complex ensemble and boosting methods can be measured, consistent with the pipeline's own Step 3 (Model Training), which starts from Logistic Regression before moving to Random Forest and XGBoost/LightGBM.

## 4. Handling Class Imbalance

Because fraud accounts for roughly 0.17% of transactions in the Kaggle dataset, imbalance-handling is the aspect of the literature most directly relevant to this project's Step 2.

**Oversampling (SMOTE and variants).** The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) remains the most commonly applied resampling method in this literature. Multiple recent studies using the same or similar datasets show that SMOTE's benefit is highly model-dependent: ensemble/tree-based methods such as Random Forest and XGBoost tend to benefit consistently and stably from SMOTE, while linear models such as Logistic Regression and SVM often show a sharp precision drop after oversampling, because the added synthetic minority samples shift the decision boundary and increase false positives (arXiv 2507.14706, 2025; arXiv 2509.19032, 2025). One evaluation on the exact Kaggle dataset found that after SMOTE, XGBoost achieved the best F1-score (0.937) and accuracy (0.941) on a balanced test set, but that when the same trained models were evaluated on the *original, imbalanced* test distribution, precision collapsed for several models (e.g., Logistic Regression precision fell to 0.06), underscoring that performance on an artificially balanced test set can be misleading and that evaluation should also be done on realistic, imbalanced holdout data (arXiv 2509.15044, 2025).

**Comparative studies across balancing strategies.** A 2024 comparative study evaluated Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost against several balancing strategies — SMOTE, ADASYN, Random Oversampling, Cluster/Neighborhood-based undersampling (NCR), and hybrids such as SMOTE+NCR — finding that Random Oversampling combined with XGBoost achieved the best F1-score (92.4%), narrowly ahead of Random Forest with Random Oversampling and Random Forest with NCR undersampling (ScienceDirect, 2024). Other work combining hybrid oversampling (SMOTE-ENN, ADASYN) with Random Forest and XGBoost reported very high accuracy (up to ~99.99%) and reduced false negatives, though such very high accuracy figures on this dataset should be interpreted cautiously given the extreme imbalance (IIETA, "Hybrid Oversampling Approach," n.d.). A further Kaggle-dataset study applying SMOTE with Random Forest and XGBoost reported Random Forest AUC of 0.978 and AUPRC of 0.871, versus XGBoost AUC of 0.983 and AUPRC of 0.867, while noting that both models carry meaningful computational overhead that could complicate real-time deployment (IIETA, "Comparative Analysis Using SMOTE," n.d.).

**Undersampling, cost-sensitive learning, and pipeline ordering.** Beyond SMOTE, class weighting (cost-sensitive learning) and random undersampling remain common alternatives, particularly because they avoid introducing synthetic data. One study examined the effect of *when* resampling is applied relative to the train/test split, showing that a baseline XGBoost model with regularization and no explicit imbalance handling, together with cost-sensitive learning (via `scale_pos_weight`), could match or exceed resampled variants — precision 97.5%, recall 91.5%, F1 94.3% without resampling versus roughly 92–93% F1 with various SMOTE/Random-Oversampling pipelines — highlighting that careful regularization and class weighting can sometimes outperform synthetic resampling altogether, and that resampling before versus after the train/test split materially affects reported performance due to data leakage risk (arXiv 2412.07437, 2024). This is directly relevant to the project's Step 1 emphasis on a stratified train/test split performed before any resampling.

## 5. Ensemble and Boosting Methods

Across nearly all of the comparative literature reviewed, tree-based ensembles — Random Forest, XGBoost, and increasingly LightGBM and CatBoost — consistently outperform single classifiers and linear models on this dataset. A recent review of ML-based financial fraud detection frameworks specifically identified ensemble methods as particularly effective relative to standalone classifiers, a conclusion echoed by later work showing that combining decision trees, random forests, and logistic regression in ensembles consistently outperforms individual models (Frontiers in AI, 2025, citing Ali et al., 2022; Shah & Sharma, 2023; Salunke et al., 2025). Work extending boosting to credit-risk-adjacent tasks similarly found that Deep Neural Networks combined with hybrid resampling (SMOTETomek) achieved the strongest results (94.9% F1, 98% ROC-AUC) among a field that included Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost (ResearchGate, "Integrating SMOTE with XGBoost," 2026 preprint). This body of evidence supports the project's choice of XGBoost/LightGBM as the expected best performers among the models proposed in Step 3.

## 6. Deep Learning Approaches

While tree-based ensembles dominate tabular benchmarks on this dataset, a growing strand of literature explores deep learning, particularly for capturing sequential or temporal transaction patterns that PCA-transformed tabular features cannot easily express. LSTM networks have been proposed specifically to model sequences of a cardholder's transactions rather than scoring each transaction independently, on the reasoning that fraud often manifests as an anomalous *pattern* over time rather than a single anomalous transaction (GTU repository, 2025). Autoencoders are also used in an unsupervised or semi-supervised capacity: the model is trained to reconstruct genuine transactions, and transactions with high reconstruction error are flagged as potentially fraudulent, which is useful when labeled fraud examples are scarce or when the goal is to catch novel fraud patterns not present in the training data (heka.ai, n.d.). Industry-facing discussions of real-time fraud systems describe a hybrid architecture pattern — feedforward networks for per-transaction scoring, LSTMs for sequential behavior, and autoencoders for anomaly detection — combined with deterministic rule-based safeguards for edge cases, reflecting the reality that pure deep learning is rarely deployed without complementary rule- or ensemble-based checks in production systems (OMSCS conference abstract, 2026). These approaches map directly onto the "Optional, more advanced" neural network step and the "simulate real-time streaming" extension suggested in this project's plan.

## 7. Explainability (SHAP and Related Methods)

Because a flagged transaction can result in a blocked card or a declined purchase, explainability has become a recurring theme rather than an optional add-on. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is the most commonly applied technique, used to attribute a fraud prediction to the specific input features (e.g., transaction amount, time-of-day-derived features, or the anonymized V-features) that drove it. One applied discussion of this exact Kaggle dataset walked through using SHAP alongside a Random Forest model to explain why particular features (commonly cited as V14, V12, and V10 in this dataset) drove individual fraud alerts, arguing that feature-importance rankings alone are insufficient for investigators who need to understand *why a specific transaction* was flagged (Scratch & Win blog, n.d.). SHAP has also been extended to deep learning models via DeepExplainer, including work that combines autoencoder reconstruction error with SHAP to explain *which* input features contributed most to an anomalous reconstruction, effectively adding local interpretability to an otherwise unsupervised detector (heka.ai, n.d.). More recent work has gone a step further, using SHAP not just for post-hoc explanation but to guide the class-rebalancing process itself, i.e., using feature attributions to decide which synthetic minority samples are most informative to generate (ScienceDirect, 2024). Applied industry pieces similarly frame the combination of gradient-boosted models (e.g., XGBoost) with SHAP as a practical way to reconcile predictive accuracy with the transparency that banking regulators and fraud analysts require (DZone, "Banking Fraud Prevention," n.d.). This literature directly supports the "Possible Extensions" section of this project, which proposes adding SHAP-based explainability for real fraud analysts.

## 8. Evaluation Metrics in the Literature

A near-universal finding across this literature is that raw accuracy is misleading on this dataset because of the extreme imbalance — a naive "always predict genuine" classifier would already exceed 99.8% accuracy while catching zero fraud. Consistent with this, the comparative studies reviewed above consistently foreground Precision, Recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and — increasingly — Precision-Recall AUC (AUPRC) as the metrics of record, since AUPRC is widely regarded in this literature as more informative than ROC-AUC specifically under severe class imbalance (IIETA, "Comparative Analysis Using SMOTE," n.d.). Several studies also explicitly report performance separately on an artificially balanced test set versus the original imbalanced test distribution, since the two can diverge sharply, reinforcing that final evaluation should always be performed on the untouched, imbalanced test split (arXiv 2509.15044, 2025). This directly validates the metric choices already specified in Step 4 of this project's pipeline.

## 9. Summary of Open Challenges Identified in the Literature

- **Class imbalance remains the central challenge**, and no single resampling technique dominates across all model families; the effect of SMOTE, ADASYN, random oversampling, and undersampling is highly model-dependent (tree ensembles benefit more consistently than linear models).
- **Reported near-perfect accuracy figures should be treated cautiously**; several studies show that performance on a synthetically balanced test set does not reflect performance on the real, imbalanced distribution the model will face in production.
- **Concept drift** — the tendency for fraud patterns to change over time — is repeatedly cited as a limitation of static, one-time-trained models, motivating periodic retraining.
- **Explainability is increasingly treated as a deployment requirement, not a nice-to-have**, particularly for gradient-boosted and deep learning models, with SHAP the dominant tool used across the literature.
- **Deep learning (LSTM, autoencoders) is promising for capturing sequential/behavioral fraud patterns** but is more often proposed in hybrid combination with rule-based safeguards and ensemble models than as a standalone replacement for them, especially where low-latency real-time scoring is required.
